1 · Combine with Experimental Evidence

Single antibodies can cause disease. Using a HuProt protein microarray, Lanz et al. found that a monoclonal antibody isolated from a multiple sclerosis patient binds the human protein HEPACAM (GlialCAM). Molecular mimicry with Epstein-Barr virus is thought to underlie this cross-reactivity. The calculation walkthrough explains how this match is mapped.

Here, we compare the HEPACAM sequence from a HuProt protein microarray experiment (JHU19101; Q14CZ8) with viral proteins and polyproteins from Curated Viral Proteomes (120). The known 5/9 match is present but does not stand out by sequence similarity alone. The authors knew to focus on EBV due to recent work connecting it with MS, and had separate PhIP-Seq experiments with this same antibody demonstrating binding to proline-rich peptides similar to this match site within HEPACAM. They distinguished this site further by showing phosphorylation of GlialCAM serine 376 (pSer376) increased binding affinity of the tested antibody about 50-fold.

2 · Discover Mimicry Across Proteomes

Viruses can evolve molecular mimics of host proteins. Comparing coverage in both directions helps distinguish similarity spanning much of a protein from smaller, localized matches.

Here, the target is human IL10, an immunoregulatory cytokine. Epstein-Barr virus encodes an evolved homolog, BCRF1, that mimics human IL10 activity. Other viruses, including human cytomegalovirus, also encode functional IL10 mimics. The full extent and mechanisms of viral mimicry remain incompletely understood; these comparisons highlight candidates for investigation, including less extensive similarities whose biological significance is unknown.

3 · Find Uniqueness Within Proteomes

Avoiding unwanted cross-reactivity is important for drug design and the selection of monoclonal antibody reagents. However, distinctive regions can be difficult to find. The mapping system described here identifies regions unmatched within a chosen comparison population, such as across the entire human proteome.

In this example, IGHG2, the constant region of the IgG2 heavy chain, is compared with the human proteome at ≥8/9 core identity. Only one short stretch is unmatched at this threshold; the remaining regions closely resemble other IgG subclasses such as IgG1, IgG3, and IgG4.
